# How to keep improving the model

You trained a 0.2B transformer that imitates an entropy solver playing Wordle. Imitation caps you at the teacher's strength. The teacher wins ~99% of games, so a perfect student wins ~99%. Everything below is how you get past that ceiling, ordered by effort. Each section says what to do, why it works, and where in this repo to do it.

## The honest number first

Two numbers matter: solve rate on answers the model has seen as secrets, and solve rate on a held-out set it has never seen. The second one is the real skill. The repo already splits 200 answers out of training (`split_answers` in `src/wordle02b/evaluate.py`), and every evaluation in the notebooks uses them. If you report one number, report the holdout one.

Confidence intervals: at 300 games, a solve rate of 0.95 has a 95% CI of roughly ±2.5 points. At 1000 games it's ±1.4 points. Differences under 2-3 points are noise at 300 games. Judge with the right sample size, or don't judge.

## Level 0 — free fixes (do these first)

### Consistency filter

A guess that contradicts the feedback you've already seen can never be the secret. Blocking those guesses costs nothing and removes a whole class of silly losses. This is on by default in `next_guesses` (`consistent=True`). If you turn it off for an ablation, expect a small drop.

### Repeat suppression

Already implemented: the model is never allowed to repeat a guess. Repeating a non-winning word is always a wasted move.

### Fix the evaluation protocol before you change anything else

Seed everything, same secrets for every contestant, report guess distributions not just solve rate. The notebooks do this. Keep it that way when you experiment, or your "improvements" will be measuring noise.

## Level 1 — more and better data (low effort, real gains)

### More games

300k games is ~7M tokens. A 0.2B model is hungry; 1-2M games is a reasonable next stop. Data generation is embarrassingly parallel (`scripts/generate_data.py --games 1000000 --workers 16`). The model will keep eating until it saturates. Watch the holdout solve rate per checkpoint and stop when it flattens.

### More exploration in the teacher

The teacher currently picks the argmax entropy guess 80% of the time. The variety in the data comes from the other 20%. Raise `explore` to 0.4-0.5 in `EntropySolver` and regenerate. More diverse trajectories = a model that has seen more game states = better generalization to the states it has to play from at test time. This is the cheapest quality lever there is.

### Better openers

30% of games start from a random word, which teaches the model to dig out of bad starts. That's useful, but it also dilutes the data with weak openers. Try: 50% entropy opener, 30% random, 20% fixed strong opener ("soare"-class words). The model should see good openers often enough to learn them, and bad openers often enough to learn recovery.

### Hard-mode data

Wordle's hard mode forbids guesses that ignore known feedback. The consistency filter already enforces that at inference, but training data generated by the teacher occasionally contains inconsistent exploratory guesses. Generate a hard-mode dataset (teacher restricted to consistent guesses) and mix it in. The model gets cleaner patterns to learn from.

### A better teacher

The teacher's strength is the student's ceiling. Two upgrades:

1. Full entropy over all 12,972 guesses instead of a sampled pool of 300. The first move in particular benefits: the best openers are often non-answers, and a 300-word sample rarely finds them. Cost: ~10x slower data generation. Worth it.
2. An exact solver. The information-theoretic optimum (minimax over the candidate tree) solves the official list at ~100% in ~3.4 average guesses, and precomputed decision trees exist for this exact corpus. Imitating near-perfect play raises the ceiling you're chasing. This is the highest-effort data lever but also the highest-ceiling one. A cheap middle ground: use the exact solver's first 2 moves (precomputed opening table) and the entropy solver after.

### Data hygiene

- Balance: each answer appears as a secret roughly equally often (uniform sampling already does this). Don't let a few words dominate.
- Dedupe: with low exploration, the same trajectory repeats. `explore` fixes this better than dedup does.
- Include losses: the teacher almost never loses, so the model rarely learns to recover from a bad spot. Generate a small dataset with a deliberately weak policy (e.g. `explore=0.9`) so the model sees losing games and what to do after them.

## Level 2 — architecture (medium effort)

### Letter-level tokens

Right now each word is one token. The model has to reverse-engineer letter structure from 12,972 opaque embeddings. That's a lot of the difficulty. Give it the letters directly.

Two ways:

1. Full letter tokenizer: each guess becomes 5 letter tokens + 5 feedback tokens. Vocab drops to ~35 tokens, sequences grow to ~42. The model learns letter patterns (doubles, common digraphs, positional constraints) that transfer across words.
2. Hybrid (cheaper): keep the word token but concatenate per-position letter embeddings to the word embedding at that position. One token per guess, letter signal injected. Try this first — it's a 10-line change in `model.py` and often a 2-5 point solve-rate jump.

### Bigger model

0.2B → 0.5B with more data buys a few points, but only if data scales with it. A 0.2B model is not the bottleneck at 300k games; the teacher's trajectory diversity is. Fix the data first, scale later.

### Pretrained warm start

Instead of training from scratch, initialize from a small pretrained LM (letter-level tokenizer, e.g. a GPT-2-class model on English). The model starts knowing letter structure and word frequency, and you fine-tune the Wordle head. Faster convergence, better low-data behavior. HuggingFace `transformers` makes this straightforward if you want to go that route — the `model.py` GPT is self-contained on purpose, so you can swap it without touching the rest of the pipeline.

## Level 3 — training dynamics (medium-high effort, the real ceiling-breaker)

### Self-play distillation (expert iteration, the easy RL)

The model trained on teacher data plays worse than the teacher. Fix that by feeding it its own good games:

1. Take the current model, play N games with exploration (temperature 0.7-1.0 sampling).
2. Keep only games the model won, and only the moves from those games.
3. Mix 20-50% of these self-generated samples into the next training run.
4. Repeat.

Why it works: the data now contains trajectories the model can actually reproduce, so its own policy gets denser support. This is how AlphaGo went from imitating humans to beating them. In this repo: generate games with `play_games(..., temperature=0.8)`, filter by `game.solved`, encode with the same tokenizer, append to the stream, retrain. Two or three rounds of this usually closes most of the gap to the teacher.

### Policy gradient (REINFORCE with baseline)

The reward is sparse but cheap to sample: a solved game is +1, minus a small penalty per extra guess (-0.05/guess works). Roll out games with the current policy, then do a policy-gradient step weighted by (reward - batch mean reward).

```
for batch of games:
    play to the end with the policy, recording log-probs of each guess
    reward = solved ? 1.0 - 0.05*(guesses-3) : 0.0
    advantage = reward - mean(rewards in batch)
    loss = -sum(log_prob * advantage)
```

The notebook training loop (`train.py`) is one function away from this: instead of teacher targets, use the model's own rollouts. This is the only lever in this list that directly optimizes winning the game rather than imitating someone who wins. Expect it to beat the teacher's average guesses, and eventually its solve rate.

### Reward shaping

All solved games are not equal. Solving in 2 is better than solving in 6. The -0.05/guess penalty above does this cheaply. A stronger version: reward only games solved in ≤3 guesses, so the model is pushed toward the teacher's sharpest play. Watch out: too harsh and the model plays recklessly to finish fast and loses more. Tune the penalty.

### MCTS / tree search in the loop

The full search space is 12,972 words and 6 turns — tiny by board-game standards. A minimax/MCTS hybrid that uses the model as a policy heuristic will beat both the model alone and the greedy teacher:

- For a candidate next guess, simulate the game forward for each possible feedback pattern (at most 243, usually fewer than 10 survive with real candidates).
- Use the model to finish the game from each resulting state (or use a learned value estimate).
- Pick the guess with the best expected outcome.

This is exactly what the exact solvers do, with the model standing in for the exhaustive tree. Expected result: solve rates in the 99-100% range, because you're no longer trusting the model's single next guess — you're searching. `benchmarks/api_benchmark.py` already gives you the harness to measure it.

## Level 4 — inference-time tricks

### Ensemble

Average the logits of 2-3 checkpoints from different seeds. Cheap robustness; usually +0.5-1.5 points. The model is small enough that running 3 of them is still faster than one API call.

### Teacher hybrid

At test time, check agreement: if the model's top-1 is within the teacher's top-3 (or the teacher agrees), play it; otherwise fall back to the teacher's pick. This "trust but verify" layer closes most of the imitation tax with zero retraining. You have both players in the same process — the pattern matrix is already in memory.

### Look-ahead on the last moves

When there are 2-3 candidates left, the model's single-shot guess is less reliable than brute force: enumerate the remaining consistent words and pick the one that eliminates the most (entropy, using the pattern matrix). A 20-line function, and it fixes the most common endgame losses. The consistency machinery in `evaluate.py` already does half the work.

## What to actually do next (roadmap)

| Lever | Effort | Expected gain |
|---|---|---|
| Consistency filter + holdout eval | done | honest numbers, removes silly losses |
| More games (1M) + explore=0.4 | 1 day | +1-3% solve |
| Letter-feature hybrid embeddings | half a day | +2-5% solve |
| Endgame candidate enumeration | half a day | fewer endgame losses |
| Teacher hybrid at inference | 1 day | most of the imitation tax |
| Self-play distillation, 2-3 rounds | 2-3 days | closes gap to teacher |
| Policy gradient | 3-5 days | beats teacher's avg guesses |
| Exact-solver teacher | 2-4 days | raises the ceiling |
| MCTS hybrid | 1-2 weeks | 99-100% solve |

## The trap to avoid

Don't tune on the training distribution. The model can memorize trajectories for answers it has seen as secrets — that inflates "solve rate" without teaching it anything. That's exactly why the holdout split exists. Every experiment report should quote the holdout number. If you quote the other one, you're lying to yourself about how good the model is.

And don't confuse solve rate with skill. The teacher wins 99% of games; an exact solver wins ~100% in fewer guesses. The interesting frontier past ~99% is average guesses, not wins. That's where the RL and search levers live.
